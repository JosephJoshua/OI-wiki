Many concepts in 3D geometry are the same as in [2D geometry](./2d.md), and we can solve 3D geometry problems with the same methods we use for 2D ones.

## Basic concepts

Points, vectors and lines work the same way as in 2D geometry, so we won't go into them again here.

### Planes

We can represent a plane by a point $P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ on the plane together with a normal vector $\boldsymbol{n}$ of the plane (a vector perpendicular to the plane).

Since $\boldsymbol{n}$ is perpendicular to the plane, it is perpendicular to every line in the plane. In other words, let $\boldsymbol{n}=(A,B,C)$; then every point $P(x,y,z)$ on the plane satisfies $\boldsymbol{n} \cdot \overrightarrow{PP_0} = 0$.

By the definition of the dot product, this is equivalent to:

$$
A(x-x_0)+B(y-y_0)+C(z-z_0)=0
$$

Rearranging gives:

$$
Ax+By+Cz-(Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0)=0
$$

Let $D=-(Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0)$. Then the equation becomes $Ax+By+Cz+D=0$. We call this the **general form** of a plane.

## Basic operations

### Angles between lines and planes

With space vectors, the angles between lines and planes in space can be found quickly.

For two skew lines $a$ and $b$, take a point $P$ in space and draw $a' \parallel a$ and $b' \parallel b'$ through it. The acute or right angle between $a'$ and $b'$ is called the **angle between the skew lines** $a$ and $b$.

For a line $a$ and a plane $\alpha$, suppose $a$ meets $\alpha$ at $A$. From a point $P$ on $a$, drop a perpendicular to $\alpha$ that meets $\alpha$ at $O$. The complement of the angle between $a$ and $PO$ is called the **angle between the line and the plane**. In particular, if $a \parallel \alpha$ or $a \subset \alpha$, the angle between them is $0^\circ$.

For two planes $\alpha$ and $\beta$, their angle is defined as the angle between two lines $a,b$ (with $a \subset \alpha$, $b \subset \beta$) that are both perpendicular to the line of intersection $l$ of the two planes.

#### Angle between two lines, and conditions for their relationships

-   The angle between the direction vectors of two lines is called the angle between the two lines.

From this, we get the following result: given two lines $l_1, l_2$ with direction vectors $s_1 (m_1, n_1, p_1)$ and $s_2 (m_2, n_2, p_2)$, and letting $\varphi$ be the angle between them, we have $\cos \varphi = \dfrac{\left | m_1m_2+n_1n_2+p_1p_2 \right |}{\sqrt{m_1^2+n_1^2+p_1^2}\sqrt{m_2^2+n_2^2+p_2^2}}$.

-   $l_1 \perp l_2 \iff m_1m_2 + n_1n_2 + p_1p_2 = 0$

-   $l_1 \parallel l_2 \iff \dfrac{m_1}{m_2} = \dfrac{n_1}{n_2} = \dfrac{p_1}{p_2}$.

### Angle between a 3D vector and a plane

When a line is not perpendicular to a plane, the angle $\varphi$ ($\varphi \in [0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$) between the line and its projection onto the plane is called the angle between the line and the plane.

Let the line's direction vector be $s(m, n, p)$ and the plane's normal vector be $f(a, b, c)$. Then the following hold:

-   Sine of the angle: $\sin\varphi = \dfrac{\left | am + bn + cp \right |}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}\sqrt{m^2+n^2+p^2}}$

-   The line is parallel to the plane $\iff am+bn+cp = 0$

-   The line is perpendicular to the plane $\iff \dfrac{a}{m} = \dfrac{b}{n} = \dfrac{c}{p}$

## Theorems of solid geometry

### Three sines theorem

Let the dihedral angle $M-AB-N$ have measure $\alpha$. In plane $M$ there is a ray $AC$ that makes an angle $\beta$ with the edge $AB$ and an angle $\gamma$ with plane $N$. Then $\sin\gamma = \sin\alpha\cdot\sin\beta$.

### Three cosines theorem

Let $O$ be a point in a plane, let $B$ be a point outside the plane, let $AO$ be the projection of line $BO$ onto the plane, and let $OC$ be a line in the plane. Then the cosines of the three angles $\angle COB, \angle AOC, \angle AOB$ satisfy $\cos\angle BOC=\cos\angle AOB\cdot\cos\angle AOC$ ($\angle AOC$ and $\angle AOB$ must be acute).
