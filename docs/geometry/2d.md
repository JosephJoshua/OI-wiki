Here we restrict the geometry problems we want to solve to the two-dimensional plane, which brings in the basics of 2D computational geometry. When studying such problems, we usually place the figures in a Cartesian coordinate system or a polar coordinate system, which makes the problems much easier to solve.

## Prerequisites

If you are not familiar with:

-   basic geometry
-   the Cartesian coordinate system
-   vectors (including the cross product)
-   polar coordinates and the polar coordinate system

## Representing figures

### Points

In a Cartesian coordinate system, a point is given by its x- and y-coordinates, for example the point $(5,2)$ or the point $(-1,0)$. Since coordinates are ordered pairs, you can store them with [`std::pair`](https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/utility/pair.html), or define a struct for them.

In a polar coordinate system, a point is given by its radius and polar angle. You can store it the same way as in the Cartesian case.

### Vectors

A vector is written with coordinates just like a point, so you can store a vector the same way you store a point.

The same goes for the polar coordinate system.

### Lines

Representing lines in polar coordinates is fairly hard, so we only discuss the Cartesian case.

#### Lines and rays

When solving math problems, we usually describe a line with an equation. There are many forms: the general form $Ax+By+C=0$, the slope-intercept form $y=kx+b$, the intercept form $\dfrac{x}{a}+\dfrac{y}{b}=1$, and so on. These forms are all meant for solving equations analytically. In many computational geometry problems, describing a line with an equation only makes the problem harder.

In computational geometry, a line is usually represented by one point on it together with its direction vector, and a ray by its endpoint together with its direction vector. A direction vector is a nonzero vector parallel to the line, and we usually take a unit vector. There are two unit vectors parallel to a given line, and you may choose either one depending on the problem; for a ray, the direction vector usually points the same way the ray extends.

#### Line segments

To store a line segment, just store its two endpoints.

### Polygons

Store the vertices of the polygon in an array in some order. For a simple polygon, the vertices are usually stored in clockwise or counterclockwise order.

As a special case, if a rectangle has all sides parallel to the coordinate axes, you only need to store the two vertices on one of its diagonals.

### Curved figures

For some special curves, such as graphs of functions, we usually store their equations. For a circle, just store its center and radius.

## Basic formulas

### Law of sines

In $\triangle \text{ABC}$, let the sides opposite angles $A,B,C$ be $a,b,c$. Then:

$$
\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}=2R
$$

Here $R$ is the radius of the circumcircle of $\triangle \text{ABC}$.

### Law of cosines

In $\triangle \text{ABC}$, let the sides opposite angles $A,B,C$ be $a,b,c$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^2&=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A\\
b^2&=a^2+c^2-2ac\cos B\\
c^2&=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C
\end{aligned}
$$

We skip the proofs of these formulas. Both are part of the standard Chinese high school math curriculum (PEP edition A, Required Course 2; Required Course 5 in the old textbooks).

## Basic operations

### Which side of a line a point is on

We have a point $P$ on a line and the line's direction vector $\mathbf v$, and we want to know which side of the line some point $Q$ is on. Here "which side" is relative to the direction vector: we decide it while facing the direction the vector points.

Using the properties of the cross product, compute $\mathbf v\times \overrightarrow {PQ}$. By the right-hand rule, if the cross product is negative, $Q$ is on the right of the line; if it is $0$, $Q$ is on the line; if it is positive, $Q$ is on the left of the line.

### Bounding-box test and straddle test

Now we want to decide whether two line segments intersect.

First, handle some special cases. If the two segments are parallel, they naturally cannot intersect (in the usual sense). You can detect this by checking whether the lines containing the segments have equal slopes.

Of course, if the two segments overlap fully or partly, or if they meet at an endpoint of one of them, you only need to check whether three of the points are collinear.

There are also cases where the segments clearly do not intersect, which we informally describe as "the two segments are too far apart". Define "the region of a segment" as the axis-parallel rectangle that has the segment as its diagonal. Then you can see that if the regions of two segments do not overlap, the two segments definitely do not intersect.

For example, take these two segments:

Their regions look like this:

![Seg2](./images/2d-seg2.svg)

So we can quickly tell that these two segments do not intersect.

This is the **bounding-box test** (also called the rapid rejection test). The case above is said to **fail the bounding-box test**.

Failing the bounding-box test is a **sufficient but not necessary condition** for the two segments to have no common point, so we need a further check.

If two segments $a,b$ intersect, the two endpoints of $b$ must lie on different sides of the line containing $a$; likewise, the two endpoints of $a$ must lie on different sides of the line containing $b$. We can directly check where the two endpoints of one segment lie relative to the line containing the other segment: if they are on different sides, the segments intersect, otherwise they do not. As explained in the previous section, the position of a point relative to a line can be found with the cross product.

This is the **straddle test**. For two segments $a,b$, if the two endpoints of $b$ lie on both sides of the line containing $a$, **and** the two endpoints of $a$ lie on both sides of the line containing $b$, we say that $a,b$ **pass the straddle test**, which means the two segments intersect.

Note that two collinear segments that do not intersect can also pass the straddle test, so to get an exact answer you need to combine it with the bounding-box test.

### Whether a point is inside a polygon

In computational geometry this is called the [PIP problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_in_polygon). There are several well-known methods for it, introduced one by one below.

#### Ray casting algorithm

You can see the original idea [here](https://wrfranklin.org/Research/Short_Notes/pnpoly.html).

First handle some special cases, such as "the point is too far from the polygon". As with checking whether two segments intersect, consider the smallest rectangle that fully covers the polygon. If the point is not inside this rectangle, it is definitely not inside the polygon. This rectangle is easy to find: take the minimum and maximum x- and y-coordinates of the polygon, and pair them up to get four points, which are the four corners of the rectangle.

The case where the point lies on an edge or a vertex of the polygon is also easy to check: for each edge, test whether the point lies on it or on its endpoints.

Now draw a ray starting from the point. If the ray crosses the polygon an odd number of times, the point is inside the polygon; otherwise it is outside. We remember this as **odd inside, even outside**. This algorithm is also called the even-odd rule.

By the [Jordan curve theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve_theorem), every time the ray crosses an edge of the polygon, it switches between inside and outside. So we only need the parity of the number of crossings.

For the ray, you can choose the slope of its line at random. An irrational slope is recommended, to avoid the ray overlapping an edge of the polygon.

The original code uses the last point in the polygon's vertex array as a point on the ray. When counting, if the ray passes through an edge or a vertex of the polygon, you can treat the points the ray passes through as lying on one side of the ray, and then just do the straddle test.

???+ warning "Note"
    The ray casting algorithm only works for simple polygons. The Jordan curve theorem does not apply to self-intersecting polygons, so the algorithm cannot decide those.

#### Winding number algorithm

The winding number is a mathematical concept: it is the total number of times a closed curve in the plane goes counterclockwise around a point. It is easy to see that when the winding number is $0$, the point is outside the curve. This algorithm is also called the nonzero rule.

How do we compute it? Connect the point to every vertex of the polygon, and add up the angles between consecutive connecting segments. Note that these angles are **signed**. If the sum is $0$, the point is outside the polygon; otherwise it is inside.

### Intersection point of two lines

First we need to make sure the two lines intersect. Just check whether their direction vectors are parallel. If they are, the lines are parallel and have $0$ intersection points. Going further, if the two lines are parallel and pass through a common point, they coincide.

So the problem reduces to this: lines $AB$ and $CD$ meet at one point, and we want to find that intersection point $E$.

If two lines intersect, there is exactly one intersection point. Since we store a point on the line and its direction vector, we only need the distance $l$ from that point to the intersection point; then we move the point $l$ units along the direction vector.

Build a triangle and use the law of sines to find $l$. The cross product lets us express the law of sines.

![Intersection](./images/2d-intersection.svg)

From the figure, $|\mathbf a\times \mathbf b|=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin \beta$ and $|\mathbf u\times \mathbf b|=|\mathbf u||\mathbf b|\sin \theta$.

Dividing the two gives:

$$
T=\frac{|\mathbf u\times \mathbf b|}{|\mathbf a\times \mathbf b|}=\frac{|\mathbf u|\sin \theta}{|\mathbf a|\sin \beta}
$$

We can see that $\left|\dfrac{|\mathbf u|\sin \theta}{\sin \beta}\right|=l$. If the expression inside the absolute value is positive, we move in the direction of $\mathbf a$; otherwise we move in the opposite direction.

Also, if we multiply $T$ directly by $\mathbf a$, the unit vector of the line appears automatically, so no further cancellation is needed.

So the intersection point is simply point $B$ minus $T\mathbf a$.

### Perimeter and area of any polygon

#### Perimeter of any polygon

Just compute it directly. Simplicity is a virtue.

#### Area of any polygon

Using the geometric meaning of the magnitude of the cross product, we can compute the area with cross products.

Label the vertices of the polygon counterclockwise as $p_1,p_2,\cdots ,p_n$, pick any auxiliary point $O$, and let $\mathbf v_i=p_i-O$. Then the area $S$ of the polygon is:

$$
S=\frac{1}{2}\left|\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf v_i\times \mathbf v_{(i\bmod n)+1}\right|
$$

### Circles and lines

#### Intersection points of a line and a circle

First find how the line and the circle are positioned relative to each other. If the line misses the circle, there is no intersection. If it is tangent, use the fact that the radius to the tangent point is perpendicular to the tangent line: this reduces the problem to finding the intersection of two lines.

If there are two intersection points, use the Pythagorean theorem to find the midpoint of the two points, then add half the chord length along the line in each direction.

#### Intersection points of two circles

First find how the two circles are positioned. If one lies completely outside or completely inside the other, there are no intersection points. If they are tangent, compute the direction vector of the line through the two centers, use the two radii to get the distance to move, and move a center along that direction vector.

If the two circles intersect, there are exactly two intersection points, and they are symmetric about the line through the two centers. So below we only explain how to find one of them; the other is found in a similar way.

Connect the center of one circle to the intersection point, and find the angle between this segment and the line through the two centers. Rotating the direction vector of the center line by this angle gives the direction vector of the radius from the center to the intersection point. Moving the center by one radius along this direction gives an intersection point.

## Polar angle order

Usually, this kind of problem asks you to first enumerate a pole, then compute the polar coordinates of all other points, and solve the problem in polar angle order in that polar coordinate system.

???+ example "[JOISC 2014 Day4 — Constellation of Two People](https://loj.ac/p/2882)"
    There are $n$ points in the plane, each colored with one of three colors. Count the pairs of non-intersecting triangles, where each triangle has one vertex of each color. $6\le n\le 3000$.

??? note "Solution"
    If two triangles do not intersect, you can always draw two internal common tangent lines between them; if they intersect or one contains the other, no internal common tangent exists. Common tangents of triangles are analogous to common tangents of circles.
    
    First enumerate an origin $O$. Use it as the pole, take the line through it parallel to the $x$-axis as the polar axis, build a polar coordinate system, and sort the remaining points by polar angle from smallest to largest. Then count how many points of each color lie above and below the polar axis.
    
    Next, enumerate the common tangent by points. Let the current point be $P$; at the start, the common tangent is the polar axis. Then some common tangent must pass through $O$ and $P$. Since the common tangent does not cross either triangle, one triangle uses points above the tangent and the other must use points below it. Count the number of ways with the multiplication principle.
    
    After counting, rotate the common tangent. Then point $P$ switches between being above and below the tangent while the other points stay put, so only its position needs updating. You can see that each pair of triangles is counted $4$ times in the end, since the same common tangent is enumerated twice, so the final answer should be divided by $4$.
    
    Now the complexity. We enumerate an origin, and for each origin we sort the remaining points and then count in linear time, so the time complexity is $O(n^2\log n)$.

## Notes on writing code

Computational geometry often involves floating-point calculations with `double`, which brings precision issues and speed issues.

Some problems, such as computing the area of a triangle whose vertices all have integer coordinates, can use their special structure to do purely integer arithmetic and avoid the precision loss of floating point.

Reading and computing floating-point numbers is slower than integers, so pay attention to how the constant factor of your program affects running time.
