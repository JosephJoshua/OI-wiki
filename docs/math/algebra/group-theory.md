## Introduction

**Group theory** studies the [algebraic structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_structure) called a group.

To study the structure of groups, we need a few basic tools: subgroups, group homomorphisms and group actions. In competitive programming, the groups that come up most are groups from number theory (such as the multiplicative group of integers modulo $n$, $(\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z)^\times$) and permutation groups, so this article focuses on the related concepts. For parts of group theory not covered here, such as the structure theory of finite groups and the theory of linear representations of groups, interested readers should consult specialized books.

???+ info "Notation"
    When there is no ambiguity, this article may write $g\cdot h$ as $gh$, and may write the group $(G,\cdot)$ simply as the group $G$.

You can't understand abstract algebra without examples. As a running example for the concepts below, we discuss the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle"
    As shown in the figure, there are six different operations that map a given equilateral triangle onto itself.

    ![Symmetry group D\_6 of an equilateral triangle](../images/triangle_d6.svg)

    Here, $r$ denotes a clockwise rotation, and $s$ denotes a flip across the line from vertex $1$ to the center of the triangle. Operations are composed from right to left, so $sr$ means rotate first ($r$) and then flip ($s$). Two operations are different if and only if some vertex of the triangle ends up in a different position after the two operations.

    | Notation |          Operation         | Order | Permutation |
    | :----: | :-----------------: | :-: | :-----: |
    |   $e$  |     Identity, i.e. do nothing     |  1  |  $(1)$  |
    |   $r$  |  Rotate clockwise by $120^\circ$  |  3  | $(123)$ |
    |  $r^2$ |  Rotate clockwise by $240^\circ$  |  3  | $(132)$ |
    |   $s$  |    Flip across the line from $1$ to the center    |  2  |  $(23)$ |
    |  $sr$  | Rotate by $120^\circ$, then flip |  2  |  $(13)$ |
    | $sr^2$ | Rotate by $240^\circ$, then flip |  2  |  $(12)$ |

    It is easy to check that these operations really form a group. For example, the identity of the group is $e$, and the inverse of $sr$ is itself. Also, $D_6$ is not a commutative group; for example, you can check directly that $rs=sr^{-1}$.

    The operations listed in the table are not the only symmetry operations matching each notation. For example, "flip across the line from $2$ to the center" is also a symmetry of the triangle. It is not in the table, but its result is the same as the operation "rotate by $120^\circ$, then flip". Concepts in the table such as "order" and "permutation representation" are explained below.

## Subgroups

To understand the structure of a given group, we can first look at its substructures. A substructure of a group is a subset of the group that is still a group under the same operation. This leads to the following definition.

???+ abstract "Subgroup"
    For a group $(G,\cdot)$ and a subset $H\subseteq G$, if $(H,\cdot)$ is also a group, then $H$ is called a **subgroup** of $G$, written $H\le G$.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In $D_6$, it is easy to check that the subgroups are $\{e\}$, $\{e,s\}$, $\{e,sr\}$, $\{e,sr^2\}$, $\{e,r,r^2\}$ and $D_6$ itself, six in total. Apart from $D_6$ itself, these subgroups all have simpler structures, and they carry part of the information about the original group.

To decide whether a given subset $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup, we don't need to check the group axioms one by one: associativity holds automatically. For a subset to be a subgroup, it is enough that it is closed under the binary operation, contains the identity, and is closed under taking inverses. In fact, these conditions can be combined into one.

???+ note "Theorem (subgroup test)"
    A nonempty subset $H$ of a group $G$ is a subgroup if and only if $g^{-1}h\in H$ for all elements $g,h \in H$.

### Subgroups generated by a subset

In general, given a subset $S$ of a group $G$, start from the elements of $S$ and apply multiplication and inversion a finite number of times. The set of all results you can get forms a subgroup of $G$. This is called the subgroup generated by the subset $S$.

???+ abstract "Subgroup generated by a subset"
    For a group $G$ and a nonempty subset $S\subseteq G$, if $H$ is the smallest (with respect to inclusion) among the subgroups of $G$ that contain $S$, then $H$ is called the **subgroup generated by the subset $S$**, written $\langle S\rangle$. In particular, if $S=\{x\}$ is a single-element set, then $\langle S\rangle$ is also written $\langle x\rangle$ and is called the cyclic subgroup of the powers of the element $x$.

One can prove that for any such subset $S$, such a subgroup always exists: $\langle S\rangle$ can be constructed as the intersection of all subgroups of $G$ that contain $S$.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In $D_6$, pick the rotation $r$ and repeatedly apply it and its inverse. This gives the subgroup $\{e,r,r^2\}$, which can be written $\langle r\rangle$. Every nontrivial subgroup of $D_6$ can be generated by choosing a single operation.

Some subsets generate the whole group. Such subsets are especially important and are called generating sets of the group.

???+ abstract "Generating set of a group"
    If a subset $S\subseteq G$ of a group $(G,\cdot)$ satisfies $\langle S\rangle=G$, then $S$ is called a **generating set** of $G$. The elements of a generating set $S$ are called **generators**.

A group is trivially a generating set of itself. The more interesting case is when a generating set is much smaller than the group itself.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    You can check that $D_6=\langle s,r\rangle$. In other words, every symmetry operation of the equilateral triangle can be obtained by composing rotations and flips.

### Cyclic groups

A group generated by a single element has a very simple structure. Such a group is called a cyclic group.

???+ abstract "Cyclic group"
    For a group $G$, if there exists $x\in G$ such that $G=\langle x\rangle$, then $G$ is called a **cyclic group**.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    All nontrivial subgroups of $D_6$, as well as $\{e\}$, are cyclic groups.

One can prove that the structure of a cyclic group is determined by its size alone. If a cyclic group is infinite, it must have the same group structure as the additive group of integers $(\mathbf Z,+)$, written $C_\infty$ or $\mathbf Z$. Otherwise, let $n\in\mathbf N_+$ be the number of elements of the group; then it must have the same group structure as the additive group of residue classes of integers modulo $n$, $(\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z,+)$, written $C_n$ or $\mathbf Z_n$. Stating this result rigorously needs the concept of [group isomorphism](#group-isomorphism) introduced below, which describes precisely what it means for two groups to have the same structure.

???+ note "Classification theorem for cyclic groups"
    A finite cyclic group $G$ of size $n$ is isomorphic to $C_n$; an infinite cyclic group $G$ is isomorphic to $C_\infty$.

??? note "Proof"
    A cyclic group $G=\langle x\rangle$ can always be written as $G=\{x^n:n\in\mathbf Z\}$. If $G$ is finite, there must be natural numbers $n<m$ with $x^n=x^m$, and by the cancellation law, $x^{m-n}=e$. In that case, take the smallest positive integer $n\in\mathbf N_+$ with $x^n=e$. Then the sequence $\{x^k\}$ is a cycle of length $n$, and the elements within one period are all distinct (otherwise $n$ would not be minimal). The map $x^k\mapsto\bar k$ then gives an isomorphism $G\rightarrow\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z$, that is, $G\cong C_n$. Conversely, if $G$ is infinite, then all the elements $x^k$ are distinct, and the map $x^k\mapsto k$ gives an isomorphism $G\rightarrow\mathbf Z$, that is, $G\cong C_\infty$.

All cyclic groups are Abelian groups. The example $D_6$ in this article shows that even if all nontrivial subgroups of a group are cyclic, the group itself may not be Abelian.

### Order

The order of a group is the number of its elements. The order of an element of a group is the order of the cyclic subgroup that element generates. This gives the following definitions:

???+ abstract "Order of a group"
    The **order** of a group $G$ is the number of its elements, written $|G|$. An infinite group has infinite order.

???+ abstract "Order of an element"
    The **order** of an element $x\in G$ of a group $G$ is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $x^n=e$, written $|x|$. If no such $n$ exists, the element $x$ is said to have infinite order, written $|x|=\infty$.

The order of an element is never larger than the order of the group. In fact, we prove below that the order of an element always divides the order of the group. However, the order of a group is not always the largest order of its elements; for example, $D_6$ is a group of order six, but its elements have order at most $3$. Nor is the order of a group always the least common multiple of the orders of all its elements; for example, the Klein four-group[^klein] $V_4$ has order $4$, but it only contains elements of order $1$ and $2$.

???+ note "Theorem"
    In a finite cyclic group $C_n=\langle x\rangle$, the order of the element $x^k$ is

    $$
    \frac{n}{\gcd(k,n)}.
    $$

    In particular, the number of generators of $C_n$ is $\varphi(n)$, where $\varphi(\cdot)$ is [Euler's totient function](../number-theory/euler-totient.md).

As an application of this discussion, note that the multiplicative group of integers modulo $n$ has order $\varphi(n)$, and the order of any element $a$ of the group divides it, so we must have $a^{\varphi(n)}=1$. This is [Euler's theorem](../number-theory/fermat.md#eulers-theorem), since the elements of this group are exactly the integers coprime to $n$.

### Cosets

The elements outside a subgroup are not arranged in a random way either.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Look at the subgroup $\langle r\rangle$ of $D_6$. The remaining elements $\{s,sr,sr^2\}$ have a structure very similar to $\langle r\rangle$: each of these operations can be obtained by composing $s$ with an operation in $\langle r\rangle$. Similarly, for the subgroup $\langle s\rangle$, the remaining elements of the group split into two classes, $\{r,sr\}$ and $\{r^2,sr^2\}$, which are obtained by composing elements of $\langle s\rangle$ with $r$ and $r^2$ respectively. This phenomenon happens in general.

Given a subgroup, we can define its cosets.

???+ abstract "Coset"
    Let $G$ be a group and $H\le G$ a subgroup. The **left coset** and **right coset** of $H$ containing $g$ are the sets

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    gH &= \{gh:h\in H\},\\
    Hg &= \{hg:h\in H\}.
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    The elements of a coset are called representative elements (representatives) of the coset.

A subgroup is itself one of its cosets. For a given subgroup, all its cosets form a partition of the group; that is, the group is the disjoint union of all the cosets. A partition can always be seen as the set of equivalence classes of some equivalence relation. For the partition into left cosets, the relation is $g_1\sim g_2$ if and only if $g_1^{-1}g_2\in H$; for the partition into right cosets, the relation is $g_1\sim g_2$ if and only if $g_1g_2^{-1}\in H$.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In the language of cosets, the example above splits $D_6$ as $\langle r\rangle\cup s\langle r\rangle$ and as $\langle s\rangle\cup \langle s\rangle r \cup \langle s\rangle r^2$. The first splits the group into left cosets, and the second into right cosets. Note that there is nothing special about the choice of representatives; for example, you can check that $s\langle r\rangle=sr\langle r\rangle$. Every element of a coset is a representative of that coset.

Different cosets of the same subgroup all have the same size, equal to the size of the subgroup. Since all cosets of a subgroup form a partition of the group, the order of a finite group must be an integer multiple of the order of the subgroup. This is called Lagrange's theorem.

???+ note "Lagrange's theorem"
    For a finite group $G$ and a subgroup $H\le G$, we have $|G|=[G:H]|H|$, where $[G:H]$ is the number of left (or right) cosets of $H$ in $G$, called the **index** of the subgroup $H$ in $G$.

??? note "Proof"
    Consider the map $h\mapsto gh$, left multiplication by $g$. It and the map $h\mapsto g^{-1}h$ are inverses of each other, so both are bijections. This shows that $|H|=|gH|$ always holds.

Note that the order of an element is the order of the cyclic subgroup it generates, so the order of an element must also divide the order of the group.

### Normal subgroups

In general, the left and right cosets of a subgroup are not the same.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In $D_6$, $\langle s\rangle r=\{r,sr\}$, but $r\langle s\rangle=\{r,sr^2\}$. However, for the subgroup $\langle r\rangle$, the left and right cosets are always the same. This is because there are only two cosets in that case, and since the subgroup itself is one coset, the other one must coincide as well.

Whether the left and right cosets are the same reflects a property of the subgroup.

???+ abstract "Normal subgroup"
    Let $N\le G$ be a subgroup of $G$. If $ghg^{-1}\in N$ for all $h\in N$ and $g\in G$, or in other words $gNg^{-1}\subseteq N$ for all $g\in G$, then $N$ is called a **normal subgroup** of $G$, written $N\trianglelefteq G$.

The condition in this definition is exactly equivalent to $gN=Ng$ always holding. A group $G$ always has the trivial normal subgroups $\langle e\rangle$ and $G$ itself.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In $D_6$, $\langle s\rangle$ is not a normal subgroup, while $\langle r\rangle$ is a normal subgroup.

### Quotient groups

Normal subgroups are a very important kind of subgroup. One reason is that they let us define quotient groups.

For a group $G$ and a normal subgroup $N\trianglelefteq G$, consider the set of all cosets

$$
G/N = \{gN:g\in G\}.
$$

Here the left and right cosets are the same, so we don't need to tell them apart. Starting from the operation of $G$, we can define a binary operation $\circ$ on $G/N$ that satisfies

$$
g_1N\circ g_2N=(g_1g_2)N.
$$

One can prove that the result of this operation does not depend on the choice of representatives[^quotient]. Then $(G/N,\circ)$ really has a group structure; it is called the **quotient group** of $G$ modulo $N$. The quotient group $G/N$ is not a subgroup of $G$; each of its elements is a subset of $G$.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    In $D_6$, the meaning of the quotient group $G/\langle r\rangle$ is very clear. It amounts to ignoring, in all these symmetry operations, the angle by which the triangle is rotated, and only paying attention to whether the triangle is flipped. Composing two operations that both flip the triangle gives an operation that does not flip it; but if one operation flips the triangle and the other doesn't, the composition must flip it. "Flipped or not" also has a group structure. Ignoring the details of rotation in $D_6$ and only keeping track of whether there is a flip is, algebraically, taking the complicated group $D_6$ modulo $\langle r\rangle$ to get the quotient group $D_6/\langle r\rangle$. This reasoning does not work for the subgroup $\langle s\rangle$: if we ignore whether there is a flip, the angle of rotation can no longer be determined clearly. This is also why $G/\langle s\rangle$ does not have the structure of a quotient group.

Quotient groups can simplify a complicated group, letting us learn about the structure of the original group by looking at part of its structure. This is why quotient groups are also called **factor groups**. A group with no normal subgroups other than the trivial ones is called a **simple group**; such groups cannot be simplified into smaller groups. Like prime numbers, they are the building blocks of more complicated group structures.

## Group homomorphisms

The second way to understand the structure of a group is to compare the structures of two groups.

To compare the structures of two groups, we construct a map between them. But such a map can't be arbitrary: it has to preserve the group structure, that is, the group operation must agree before and after the map. Such a map is called a group homomorphism.

???+ abstract "Group homomorphism"
    Let $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ be a map from a group $(G,\cdot)$ to a group $(H,\odot)$. If $\varphi$ preserves the group operation, that is, $\varphi(g_1\cdot g_2)=\varphi(g_1)\odot\varphi(g_2)$ for all $g_1,g_2\in G$, then $\varphi$ is called a **homomorphism** from the group $G$ to the group $H$.

A group homomorphism must map the identity to the identity, and inverses to inverses.

???+ info "Notation"
    Below, when there is no ambiguity, we won't distinguish the notation for the operations in $G$ and $H$, and for brevity we will omit these symbols.

### Group isomorphism

For a homomorphism $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ from a group $G$ to a group $H$, a natural question is: how far does this homomorphism show that $G$ and $H$ have the same structure? To answer this, look at the image $\varphi(G)$ of $G$ under $\varphi$, which carries the structure of $G$ into $H$. On one hand, $\varphi(G)$ is a subgroup of $H$; but if $\varphi$ is not surjective, $\varphi(G)$ is not the same as $H$. On the other hand, $\varphi$ need not be injective; if it isn't, then $\varphi(G)$ only reflects part of the structure of $G$. Only when $\varphi$ is a bijection do $G$ and $H$ have exactly the same structure. This special kind of group homomorphism is called a group isomorphism.

???+ abstract "Group isomorphism"
    Let $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ be a homomorphism from a group $G$ to a group $H$. If $\varphi$ is a bijection, then $\varphi$ is called an **isomorphism** between $G$ and $H$, written $G\cong H$.

Two isomorphic groups have exactly the same structure. If we only care about the structure of groups, there is no need to tell two isomorphic groups apart. The classification theorem for cyclic groups above is stated up to isomorphism.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Back to the earlier example: each symmetry operation of the equilateral triangle corresponds to exactly one permutation of the vertex set. All permutations of the vertex set also form a group, namely $S_3$. It is easy to check that the resulting map $\varphi:D_6\rightarrow S_3$ is a group homomorphism; moreover, it is also a group isomorphism. So $D_6\cong S_3$. In fact, a group of order six is isomorphic either to $C_6$ or to $S_3$ (see the proof below).

Classifying the structures of finite groups of a given order is an important topic in group theory, but it is beyond the scope of this article.

### Kernel of a homomorphism

For a general homomorphism, we can go further and ask how much information about the group structure is lost in the homomorphism. We keep the notation from above. We already know that $\varphi(G)$ is a subgroup of $H$; the key question now is how $\varphi(G)$ relates to $G$.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Consider the map $\varphi: D_6 \rightarrow C_2 = \langle x\rangle$ defined by:

    $$
    \varphi(e)=\varphi(r)=\varphi(r^2)=e,\ \varphi(s)=\varphi(sr)=\varphi(sr^2)=x.
    $$

    It is easy to check that $\varphi$ is a group homomorphism; it is surjective but not injective. Its meaning is clear: it maps each symmetry operation to whether or not it flips the triangle. The information in $D_6$ that this homomorphism squeezes out is the information about the rotation angle. For example, if there is no flip, a rotation by any angle is mapped to the identity of $C_2$.

This example suggests using the preimage of $\{e\}$ to measure the structural information lost in a homomorphism. This leads to the following definition.

???+ abstract "Kernel of a homomorphism"
    Let $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ be a homomorphism from a group $G$ to a group $H$. The **kernel** of $\varphi$ is $\ker\varphi=\{g\in G:\varphi(g)=e\}$, where $e$ is the identity of $H$.

### Fundamental theorem of homomorphisms

The kernel $\ker\varphi$ does indeed capture the structural information lost in a group homomorphism. The precise statement of this fact is the **fundamental theorem of group homomorphisms** (also called the **first isomorphism theorem**).

???+ note "Fundamental theorem of homomorphisms (first isomorphism theorem)"
    Let $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ be a homomorphism from a group $G$ to a group $H$. Then $\ker\varphi\trianglelefteq G$, and $G/\ker\varphi\cong\varphi(G)\le H$.

??? note "Proof"
    First, $N=\ker\varphi$ is a normal subgroup, because for any $h\in N$ we have $\varphi(ghg^{-1})=\varphi(g)\varphi(h)\varphi(g)^{-1}=\varphi(g)\varphi(g)^{-1}=e$, that is, $ghg^{-1}\in\ker\varphi$. Next, consider the map $\Phi:G/N\rightarrow\varphi(G)$ with $\Phi(gN)=\varphi(g)$. This map is well-defined, because if $g_1N=g_2N$, then $g_1^{-1}g_2\in N$, so $\varphi(g_1^{-1}g_2)=e$, that is, $\varphi(g_1)=\varphi(g_2)$. The map $\Phi$ is clearly surjective; it is also injective, because $\ker\Phi=\{gN:\varphi(g)=e\}=\{N\}$. So $\Phi$ is a group isomorphism. Finally, $\varphi(g_1)\varphi(g_2)^{-1}=\varphi(g_1g_2^{-1})\in\varphi(G)$, so by the subgroup test, $\varphi(G)$ must be a subgroup.

???+ note "Corollary"
    A homomorphism $\varphi:G\rightarrow H$ is injective if and only if $\ker\varphi=\{e\}$. In that case, $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $H$, namely $G\cong\varphi(G)\le H$.

In other words, the kernel of the homomorphism $\varphi$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, the quotient group $G/\ker\varphi$ is isomorphic to the image $\varphi(G)$ of the homomorphism, and this image is a subgroup of $H$.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    The kernel of the group homomorphism $\varphi: D_6 \rightarrow C_2$ given above is $\langle r\rangle$. When we discussed normal subgroups earlier, we already saw that $D_6/\langle r\rangle$ is indeed isomorphic to $C_2$.

### Natural homomorphisms

This result is not surprising. When we constructed the homomorphism $\varphi: D_6 \rightarrow C_2$, we used exactly the geometric meaning of the quotient group $D_6/\langle r\rangle$. This is not a coincidence. In fact, for every quotient group, we can construct a group homomorphism whose image is isomorphic to that quotient group.

???+ abstract "Natural homomorphism"
    For a group $G$ and a normal subgroup $N\trianglelefteq G$, the map $\pi: G\rightarrow G/N$ given by $\pi(g)=gN$ is a surjective homomorphism from $G$ to $G/N$. It is called the **natural homomorphism** or **natural map** from the group $G$ to the quotient group $G/N$.

This also shows that for any normal subgroup of a given group, we can find a corresponding group homomorphism whose kernel is exactly that normal subgroup. The fundamental theorem of homomorphisms above shows that the kernel of any homomorphism is a normal subgroup. So normal subgroups and kernels of homomorphisms are two sides of the same coin.

Using the natural map, the fundamental theorem of group homomorphisms really says that the following **commutative diagram** holds.

![Commutative diagram of the fundamental theorem of homomorphisms](../images/homomorphism.svg)

Here, all arrows are group homomorphisms, $N=\ker\varphi$ is the kernel of $\varphi$, and $\varphi(G)$ is the image of $\varphi$. The maps are, in order: $\pi:g\mapsto gN$, the natural map (a surjective homomorphism) from $G$ to the quotient group $G/N$; $\Phi:gN\mapsto\varphi(g)$, an isomorphism; and $\iota$, the inclusion map (an injective homomorphism). The diagram being commutative means that composing the maps along the two different paths from $G$ to $H$ gives the same result, that is, $\varphi=\iota\circ\Phi\circ\pi$. The commutative diagram shows clearly that the information lost by $\varphi$ is reflected in $\pi$ and $\iota$.

### Isomorphism theorems for groups

The isomorphism theorems are powerful tools for understanding the structure of groups. The first isomorphism theorem was given above. For completeness, here are the other common isomorphism theorems.

The second isomorphism theorem involves the product of subgroups.

???+ abstract "Product of subsets"
    For a group $G$ and subsets $A,B\subseteq G$, the **product** of $A$ and $B$ is the subset $AB=\{ab:a\in A,b\in B\}$.

The product of two subgroups is not always a subgroup. For example, in $D_6$ the product of the subgroups $A=\langle s\rangle$ and $B=\langle sr\rangle$ is $AB=\{e,s,r,sr\}$, which is not a subgroup of $G$, because $(sr)s=r^2\notin AB$. In fact, counterexamples of this form, with $a\in A$ and $b\in B$ but $ba\notin AB$, are exactly the reason a product fails to be a subgroup. This is made precise by the following theorem.

???+ note "Theorem"
    For a group $G$ and subgroups $A,B\le G$, the product $AB$ is a subgroup if and only if $AB=BA$.

??? note "Proof"
    If $AB$ is a subgroup, then $ba=(a^{-1}b^{-1})^{-1}\in AB$ for all $a\in A$ and $b\in B$, so $BA\subseteq AB$. Conversely, if $AB=BA$, then for any $a_1,a_2\in A$ and $b_1,b_2\in B$, we have $(a_1b_1)(a_2b_2)^{-1}=a_1b_1b_2^{-1}a_2^{-1}\in a_1BA=a_1AB=AB$, so by the subgroup test, $AB$ must be a subgroup.

The **second isomorphism theorem** (also called the diamond isomorphism theorem) gives a simpler sufficient condition for a product of subgroups to be a subgroup, and further determines its structure.

???+ note "Second isomorphism theorem"
    Let $G$ be a group and $A,B\le G$ subgroups with $A\le N_G(B)$. Then $AB\le G$, $B\trianglelefteq AB$, $A\cap B\trianglelefteq A$, and $AB/B\cong A/(A\cap B)$. Here $N_G(B)$ is the [normalizer](#normalizers-and-centralizers) of $B$. In particular, a sufficient condition for $A\le N_G(B)$ is $B\trianglelefteq G$.

??? note "Proof"
    Since $A\le N_G(B)$, we have $aBa^{-1}=B$ for all $a\in A$, that is, $aB=Ba$. Therefore $AB=BA$, and by the theorem above $AB$ is a subgroup. As a subgroup of $AB$, $B$ has equal left and right cosets, so $B\trianglelefteq AB$.

    Consider the map $\varphi:A\rightarrow AB/B$ with $\varphi(a)=aB$. It is surjective, and its kernel is $\ker\varphi=\{a\in A:aB=B\}=A\cap B$. Applying the fundamental theorem of homomorphisms completes the proof.

The **third isomorphism theorem** describes how the normal subgroups and quotient groups of a quotient group correspond to the normal subgroups and quotient groups of the original group. It justifies the idea of breaking a quotient group down further.

???+ note "Third isomorphism theorem"
    Let $G$ be a group with normal subgroups $H,K\trianglelefteq G$ such that $H\le K$. Then $K/H\trianglelefteq G/H$, and $(G/H)/(K/H)\cong G/K$.

??? note "Proof"
    Consider the map $\varphi:G/H\rightarrow G/K$ with $\varphi(gH)=gK$. It is a surjective group homomorphism, and $\ker\varphi=\{gH:g\in K\}=K/H$. Applying the fundamental theorem of homomorphisms completes the proof.

This result can be extended to the fourth isomorphism theorem, also called the **correspondence theorem**. It further gives a correspondence between the subgroup lattice of a group and the subgroup lattice of its quotient group.

???+ note "Correspondence theorem"
    Let $G$ be a group with a normal subgroup $N\trianglelefteq G$. Then there is a bijection $\varphi:\mathcal H\rightarrow\mathcal S$ between the set $\mathcal H=\{H:N\subseteq H\subseteq G\}$ of all subgroups of $G$ containing $N$ and the set $\mathcal S=\{S:S\le G/N\}$ of all subgroups of the quotient group $G/N$, which maps $H\in\mathcal H$ to $H/N\in\mathcal S$. This bijection preserves inclusion between subgroups, and normal subgroups of $G$ are always mapped to normal subgroups of $G/N$.

    The content and names of the isomorphism theorems may differ between textbooks. The version chosen here is a common one. ## Group actions

The third way to understand the structure of a group is to look at how the group acts on a set.

For example, the symmetry group of the equilateral triangle studied in this article is defined through how its elements (the symmetry operations) act on the triangle. As another example, the symmetric group $S_M$ can be defined through how its elements act on the set $M$. Here "act" means that each element of the group corresponds to a permutation of the set.

???+ abstract "Group action on a set"
    For a group $G$, a set $X$ and a map $G\times X\rightarrow X$, write $g\cdot x$ for the image of $(g,x)$ under this map. If the map satisfies $g_1\cdot(g_2\cdot x)=(g_1g_2)\cdot x$ and $e\cdot x=x$ for all $g_1,g_2\in G$ and $x\in X$, then the map is called a **group action** of $G$ on the set $X$.

??? info "Left actions and right actions"
    The definition of group action used here is called a left action in some places[^group-action], because in the notation $g\cdot x$ the group element is written to the left of the set element. Accordingly, when a product $g_1g_2$ of group elements acts on the set, $g_2$ acts first and then $g_1$. Of course, we can also define a right action, written $x\cdot g$. In that case the order of composition is the reverse of a left action: $x\cdot (g_1g_2)=(x\cdot g_1)\cdot g_2$. The two differ only in notation, not in substance, so this article uses the left action notation by default.

For a group action as defined above, there is a natural construction

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi:{\color{Maroon}{G}}\rightarrow {\color{Orchid}{S_X}}&\\
{\color{Maroon}{g}}\mapsto {\color{Orchid}{\varphi_g}}&: {\color{RoyalBlue}{X}}\rightarrow{\color{YellowGreen}{X}}\\
&\quad {\color{RoyalBlue}{x}}\mapsto {\color{YellowGreen}{g\cdot x}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This map sends each element $g$ of the group $G$ to a permutation $\varphi_g$ of the set $X$, and the permutation $\varphi_g$ maps the element $x$ to $g\cdot x$.

By definition, the bijection $\varphi_e$ corresponding to the identity $e$ is the identity map on $X$, and the map $\varphi_g$ for an element $g$ and the map $\varphi_{g^{-1}}$ for its inverse $g^{-1}$ are inverses of each other (this also explains why $\varphi_g$ is always a bijection). You can check that $\varphi_{g_1g_2}=\varphi_{g_1}\varphi_{g_2}$, that is, $\varphi$ is a group homomorphism from $G$ to $S_X$.

This group homomorphism $\varphi$ is called the **permutation representation** of the group action. It maps the group $G$ onto some permutation group.

???+ abstract "Permutation group"
    If a group $G$ is a subgroup of some symmetric group, then $G$ is called a **permutation group**.

The kernel of this homomorphism is also called the kernel of the group action. If the kernel is trivial, that is, the homomorphism is injective, the group action is called **faithful**, meaning its permutation representation faithfully reflects the information in the group structure. In that case, $G$ is isomorphic to the permutation group given by the permutation representation.

???+ info "Notation"
    Below, for convenience, we omit the $\cdot$ symbol in group actions.

### Orbits

A group action is a map with two arguments. If we fix an element $g$ of the group, we get the permutation $\varphi_g$ of the set. If instead we fix an element $x$ of the set, we get all the possible results of the group acting on that element.

???+ abstract "Orbit"
    For an action of a group $G$ on a set $X$ and an element $x\in X$, the **orbit** of $x$ under $G$ is the subset $Gx=\{gx:g\in G\}$.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    For example, consider the action of the group $\langle s\rangle\le D_6$ on the vertex set of the triangle. The orbit of vertex $1$ is $\{1\}$, and the orbit of vertices $2$ and $3$ is $\{2,3\}$. However, the action of the group $\langle r\rangle\le D_6$ on the vertex set has only one orbit, namely the whole vertex set.

It is easy to prove that under the action of $G$, all the orbits of the set $X$ form a partition of the set, written $X/G$. But unlike cosets, these orbits do not necessarily have the same length.

### Stabilizers

Under a group action, the length of the orbit of an element of the set depends on how many group elements have permutations that fix it.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    For example, under the action of $\langle s\rangle\le D_6$, the orbit of vertex $1$ has length one because every element of the group maps vertex $1$ to itself; the orbit of vertex $2$ has length two because only the identity $e$ maps vertex $2$ to itself.

This motivates the following definition.

???+ abstract "Stabilizer"
    For an action of a group $G$ on a set $X$ and an element $x\in X$, the **stabilizer** of $x$ in $G$ is the subgroup $G_x=\{g\in G:gx=x\}$.

The kernel of a group action is the intersection of the stabilizers of all elements of the set.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Consider the action of $D_6$ on the vertex set. The stabilizer of vertex $1$ is $\{e,s\}=\langle s\rangle$, which is a subgroup of $D_6$. Since $D_6$ splits into the left cosets $\langle s\rangle$, $r\langle s\rangle$ and $r^2\langle s\rangle$, it is easy to see that all elements of the same left coset send vertex $1$ to the same place.

This example shows that the elements of an orbit correspond one-to-one to the left cosets of the stabilizer. This gives the following result.

???+ note "Theorem"
    For an action of a group $G$ on a set $X$, the stabilizer $G_x$ of an element $x\in X$ is a subgroup of $G$, and there is a bijection between the left cosets of $G_x$ and the orbit $Gx$.

??? note "Proof"
    It is enough to check that the map $gG_x\mapsto gx$ is a well-defined bijection.

Using Lagrange's theorem, we can relate the length of an orbit to the number of cosets of the stabilizer. This is the **orbit-stabilizer theorem**.

???+ note "Orbit-stabilizer theorem"
    For an action of a finite group $G$ on a set $X$ and an element $x\in X$, we have $|Gx|=[G:G_x]=|G|/|G_x|$.

You can verify this result on the example above.

### Burnside's lemma

This lemma gives a formula for the number of orbits of a group action.

???+ note "Burnside's lemma"
    For an action of a group $G$ on a set $X$, the number of orbits equals the average number of fixed points of the permutations corresponding to the elements of the group, that is,

    $$
    |X/G| = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|X^g|.
    $$

    Here $X^g=\{x\in X:gx=x\}$ is the set of fixed points of the permutation corresponding to $g\in G$.

??? note "Proof"
    The proof of this theorem is quite short. Note that the number of orbits can be written as

    $$
    |X/G|=\sum_{o\in X/G}1=\sum_{x\in X}\frac{1}{|Gx|}=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{x\in X}|G_x|.
    $$

    The last equality is the corollary above. The right-hand side differs from what we want to prove only by Fubini's theorem: both sums count the set $\{(g,x)\in G\times X:gx=x\}$, except that the right-hand side sums over $g$ first, while the expression we want to prove sums over $x$ first.

This theorem has many uses in combinatorics. It can be used to count the number of "essentially different" objects. For more examples and discussion, see [Pólya enumeration](../combinatorics/polya.md).

### Cayley's theorem

To study the structure of a group using group actions, we need to choose a suitable set. In fact, the group itself is such a set. So next we consider two common actions of a group on itself and use them to analyze the structure of the group.

The first such action is the **left multiplication** action of a group on itself. Its permutation representation is as follows.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi:G\rightarrow S_G&\\
g\mapsto \varphi_g&: G\rightarrow G\\
&\quad x\mapsto gx
\end{aligned}
$$

The left multiplication action of a group is always faithful, because a group satisfies the cancellation law. So by the fundamental theorem of homomorphisms, $G$ can be embedded in the symmetric group $S_G$. This means every group is isomorphic to some permutation group[^cayley].

???+ note "Cayley's theorem"
    A group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group $S_G$.

This group action has only one orbit, and the stabilizer of every element is $\{e\}$.

### Conjugation action

The second action of a group on itself is called **conjugation**. Its permutation representation is as follows.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi:G\rightarrow S_G&\\
g\mapsto \varphi_g&: G\rightarrow G\\
&\quad x\mapsto gxg^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Under the conjugation action, the orbits and stabilizers have special names.

???+ abstract "Conjugacy class"
    For a group $G$ and $g\in G$, the **conjugacy class** of $g$ in $G$ is the orbit of $g$ under the conjugation action. If elements $g$ and $h$ are in the same conjugacy class, then $g$ and $h$ are said to be **conjugate**.

???+ abstract "Centralizer"
    For a group $G$ and $a\in G$, the **centralizer** of the element $a$ in $G$ is $C_G(a)=\{g\in G:ga=ag\}$.

???+ abstract "Center"
    For a group $G$, the **center** of $G$ is $Z(G)=\cap_{a\in G}C_G(a)=\{g\in G:\forall a\in G(ga=ag)\}$.

The center of a group is the set of elements that commute with every element of the group. Since it is the kernel of a group action, it must be a normal subgroup. The size of the center indicates how far the group is from being commutative. The centralizer of a given element is the set of all elements that commute with it. It is also the largest subgroup that has this element in its center, which is where its name comes from. Also, since it is the stabilizer of an element under the conjugation action, it is a subgroup. A conjugacy class is generally not a subgroup.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    Back to $D_6$: its center is $\{e\}\neq G$, which shows that it is not commutative. The centralizer $C_G(r)$ of $r$ is $\langle r\rangle$, and the centralizer $C_G(s)$ of $s$ is $\langle s\rangle$. In general, $\langle g\rangle\le C_G(g)$ always holds for all $g\in G$. The group $D_6$ has three conjugacy classes: $\{e\},\{r,r^2\},\{s,sr,sr^2\}$. It is easy to see that conjugate elements all have the same order[^conjugate].

Under the conjugation action, a group splits into a number of conjugacy classes. So we can write down the **class equation**.

???+ note "Class equation"
    For a group $G$, let $\{\mathcal K_i\}_{i=1}^r$ be all the conjugacy classes of length greater than one, and let $g_i$ be a representative of $\mathcal K_i$. Then

    $$
    |G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{i=1}^r[G:C_G(g_i)].
    $$

It can be used to analyze the structure of groups, for example to prove the [Sylow theorems](#sylow-theorems) below.

### Normalizers and centralizers

In fact, the power set of a group can also be acted on. Here we focus on the conjugation action of a group on the set of all its subsets $X=\mathcal P(G)$. Its permutation representation is as follows.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi:G\rightarrow S_X&\\
g\mapsto \varphi_g&: X\rightarrow X\\
&\quad S\mapsto gSg^{-1} = \{gsg^{-1}:s\in S\}
\end{aligned}
$$

Under this conjugation action, we can also define conjugacy classes of subsets of the group. Its kernel is still the center of the group. Its stabilizers are called normalizers.

???+ abstract "Normalizer"
    For a group $G$ and a subset $S\subseteq G$, the **normalizer** of $S$ in $G$ is $N_G(S)=\{g\in G:gSg^{-1}=S\}$.

The normalizer is the stabilizer of $S$ under the conjugation action, so it must be a subgroup of $G$. The normalizer of $S$ is the largest subgroup in which $S$ is contained in a normal subgroup, which is where the name normalizer comes from. In particular, for a single-element set $\{a\}$, $N_G(\{a\})=C_G(a)$.

The centralizer can also be extended to subsets in a similar way.

???+ abstract "Centralizer"
    For a group $G$ and a subset $S\subseteq G$, the **centralizer** of $S$ in $G$ is $C_G(S)=\{g\in G:\forall s\in S(gsg^{-1}=s)\}$.

The centralizer is actually the kernel of the conjugation action of $N_G(S)$ on $S$. So we must have $C_G(S)\trianglelefteq N_G(S)\le G$, and by transitivity of subgroups, the centralizer is also a subgroup. In particular, the center $Z(G)=C_G(G)\trianglelefteq N_G(G)=G$ of a group must be a normal subgroup.

### Sylow theorems

Going further, analyzing the conjugation action of a finite group leads to the Sylow theorems. They are a powerful tool for studying the structure of finite groups, and can quickly determine the structure of many groups of small order.

???+ abstract "$p$‑group"
    For a group $G$, if there is a prime $p$ and a positive integer $\alpha$ such that $|G|=p^\alpha$, then $G$ is called a **$p$‑group**.

??? info "About the definition of $p$‑groups"
    There is another definition of $p$‑groups: a group in which the order of every element is a power of a prime $p$. The two definitions are equivalent for finite groups, but the second also applies to infinite groups. Also, different sources disagree on whether $\{e\}$ counts as a $p$‑group, so readers should pay attention to this when reading.

???+ abstract "$p$‑subgroup"
    For a group $G$ and a subgroup $P\le G$, if $P$ is itself a $p$‑group, then $P$ is called a **$p$‑subgroup**.

???+ note "Cauchy's theorem"
    If a prime $p$ divides the order of a group $G$, then $G$ must have an element of order $p$.

Cauchy's theorem guarantees that $p$‑subgroups exist. In fact, a more careful analysis gives a stronger result: Sylow $p$‑subgroups exist.

???+ abstract "Sylow $p$‑subgroup"
    For a group $G$ and a subgroup $P\le G$, if $|G|=p^\alpha m$, $p\perp m$ and $|P|=p^\alpha$, then $P$ is called a **Sylow $p$‑subgroup**.

In other words, Sylow $p$‑subgroups are the maximal $p$‑subgroups. The Sylow theorems assert that Sylow $p$‑subgroups exist, which gives a partial converse of Lagrange's theorem.

???+ note "Sylow theorems"
    Let the order $|G|$ of a finite group $G$ be written as $p^\alpha m$, where $p$ is a prime that does not divide $m$. Then:

    1.  A Sylow $p$‑subgroup exists;
    2.  For a Sylow $p$‑subgroup $P$ and a $p$‑subgroup $Q$ of $G$, there exists $g\in G$ such that $Q\le gPg^{-1}$. In particular, all Sylow $p$‑subgroups are conjugate;
    3.  The number $n_p$ of Sylow $p$‑subgroups of $G$ satisfies $n_p\equiv 1\pmod p$, $n_p\mid m$ and $n_p=[G:N_G(P)]$, where $P$ is any Sylow $p$‑subgroup.

??? note "Proof"
    To prove the first part, use induction on $|G|$. Consider the class equation

    $$
    |G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{i=1}^r[G:C_G(g_i)].
    $$

    If $p\mid|Z(G)|$, take any subgroup $N\le Z(G)$ of order $p$ in the center. Since $N\le Z(G)$, it commutes with every element of $G$, so $N\trianglelefteq G$, and $G/N$ is a smaller group. By the induction hypothesis, it has a Sylow $p$‑subgroup $P/N$, and the corresponding preimage $P$ under the natural homomorphism is a Sylow $p$‑subgroup of the original group $G$. So we only need to consider the case where $p$ does not divide $|Z(G)|$. In this case, we can pick $g_i\in G$ such that $p$ does not divide $[G:C_G(g_i)]$, so the full power of $p$ in $|G|$ appears in $|C_G(g_i)|$. Again by the induction hypothesis, $C_G(g_i)$ has a Sylow $p$‑subgroup $P$, which is exactly a Sylow $p$‑subgroup of the original group $G$.

    To prove the second and third parts, let $\mathcal S=\{gPg^{-1}:g\in G\}$ be the conjugacy class of a Sylow $p$‑subgroup $P$ of $G$, and consider the conjugation action of $G$ on $\mathcal S$ restricted to some $p$‑subgroup $Q$. Under this action, the set $\mathcal S$ splits into a number of orbits. Suppose there are $r$ orbits in total, with representatives $P_i$. Then we have the identity

    $$
    |\mathcal S|=\sum_{i=1}^r[Q:Q\cap N_G(P_i)].
    $$

    Here $Q\cap N_G(P_i)$ is the stabilizer of $P_i$ under this group action. Let $H=Q\cap N_G(P_i)$. Clearly $H\le N_G(P_i)$, so by the second isomorphism theorem, $P_iH/P_i\cong H/(P_i\cap H)$. So $|P_iH|=|P_i||H|/|P_i\cap H|$. The right-hand side is a power of $p$, so the left-hand side must be a power of $p$ too, and $|P_i|\le |P_iH|$ must hold. But $P_i$ already has the largest power of $p$ among subgroups of $G$, so $P_i=P_iH$. Substituting into the isomorphism above gives $H=P_i\cap H=Q\cap P_i$. Here we used the obvious relation $P_i\le N_G(P_i)$. So the stabilizer $Q\cap N_G(P_i)$ is actually $Q\cap P_i$, and the equation above can be written as

    $$
    |\mathcal S|=\sum_{i=1}^r[Q:Q\cap P_i].
    $$

    This equation holds for every $p$‑subgroup $Q$.

    Of course, the Sylow $p$‑subgroup $P$ is also a $p$‑subgroup. Substituting $P$ for $Q$ in the equation above, exactly one term on the right-hand side equals $1$, and all others are multiples of $p$. So the size $|\mathcal S|$ of the conjugacy class of the Sylow $p$‑subgroup $P$ must be $1$ modulo $p$. Also, since the equation holds for any $p$‑subgroup $Q$, there must exist $g\in G$ with $Q\le gPg^{-1}$; otherwise $|Q\cap P_i|<|Q|$ for all $P_i$ in the equation, contradicting $|\mathcal S|\equiv 1\pmod p$. If we now take $Q$ to be a Sylow $p$‑subgroup different from $P$, then $Q=gPg^{-1}$ for some $g\in G$; that is, all Sylow $p$‑subgroups are conjugate. Hence the number $n_p$ of Sylow $p$‑subgroups is $|\mathcal S|$, and it must satisfy $n_p\equiv 1\pmod p$. Finally, note that $n_p=|\mathcal S|=[G:N_G(P)]$ and $P\le N_G(P)$, so $n_p\mid m$. This completes the proof of the second and third parts of the theorem.

??? example "Application: up to isomorphism, the only groups of order six are $C_6$ and $S_3$"
    Let $G$ have order $6$. By the Sylow theorems, it has a Sylow $2$‑subgroup, and their number satisfies $n_2\equiv 1\pmod 2$ and $n_2\mid 3$. So there are only two cases: $n_2=1$ or $n_2=3$. Similarly, one can show that $G$ has exactly one Sylow $3$‑subgroup, that is, $n_3=1$.

    In the case $n_2=1$, $G$ has one Sylow $2$‑subgroup, so it has one element of order $2$; it also has one Sylow $3$‑subgroup, so it has two elements of order $3$. $G$ also has an identity element. By Lagrange's theorem, the order of each remaining element must divide $6$. It can't be a new element of order $2$ or $3$, since that would give a new Sylow $p$‑subgroup different from the ones above. So the remaining elements must have order $6$. Having an element whose order equals the order of the group means $G$ is cyclic, so $G\cong C_6$.

    In the case $n_2=3$, $G$ has three conjugate Sylow $2$‑subgroups. Consider the conjugation action of $G$ on these three Sylow $2$‑subgroups. For any Sylow $2$‑subgroup $P$, the Sylow theorems give $|N_G(P)|=2$; but also $P\le N_G(P)$, so $P=N_G(P)$. Therefore the intersection of the normalizers of the three Sylow $2$‑subgroups, which is the kernel of this conjugation action, is trivial. So the action is faithful, and it embeds $G$ into the permutation group $S_3$ on these three Sylow $2$‑subgroups. But since $|G|=|S_3|$, we must have $G\cong S_3$.

## Finitely generated Abelian groups

Now that we have the basic tools for analyzing group structure, we focus on the structure of one particular class of groups.

As mentioned in the overview, Abelian groups have simpler structures than other groups because their elements commute. Among them, the especially simple ones are the Abelian groups that can be generated by finitely many elements.

???+ abstract "Finitely generated"
    If a group $G$ has a finite generating set, then $G$ is called **finitely generated**.

The classification theorem in this section shows that a finitely generated Abelian group can be seen as a simple combination of finitely many cyclic groups. The groups that appear in competitive programming are mostly finite. A finite Abelian group is always finitely generated, so this result always applies.

### Direct products

The analysis of groups above mainly focused on breaking a group down into smaller groups. Conversely, we can naturally ask how to combine two groups into a larger one. Among all possible ways of combining groups, the direct product is the simplest.

The basic idea of the direct product is this: given two groups $G$ and $H$, take their Cartesian product $G\times H$, and define the operation on pairs $(g,h)$ by operating on the two components separately, without the components affecting each other. The result is clearly a larger group, and the two original groups embed trivially into it.

???+ abstract "Direct product"
    The **direct product** of groups $(G,\cdot_G)$ and $(H,\cdot_H)$ is the group $(G\times H,\cdot)$, where the binary operation $\cdot:(G\times H)\times(G\times H)\rightarrow G\times H$ is defined by $(g_1,h_1)\cdot(g_2,h_2)=(g_1\cdot_G g_2,h_1\cdot_H h_2)$. The direct product of $G$ and $H$ is written $G\times H$.

For the direct product $G\times H$, there are trivial embeddings $g\mapsto(g,e_H)$ and $h\mapsto(e_G,h)$. Conversely, the maps $(g,h)\mapsto g$ and $(g,h)\mapsto h$ are group homomorphisms whose kernels are $\{e_G\}\times H$ and $G\times\{e_H\}$ respectively. These two kernels are exactly the images of the embeddings above, and their intersection is trivial, namely $\{(e_G,e_H)\}$. So the direct product $G\times H$ has two subgroups that are both normal, have trivial intersection, and whose product is the whole direct product $G\times H$.

This analysis actually gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a group to be the direct product of two of its subgroups.

???+ note "Theorem"
    For a group $G$ and subgroups $H_1,H_2\le G$, $G\cong H_1\times H_2$ if and only if $H_1,H_2\trianglelefteq G$, $H_1\cap H_2=\{e\}$ and $G=H_1H_2$.

??? note "Proof"
    The necessity of these conditions was discussed in the main text; here we prove that they are sufficient. Consider the map $\varphi:G\rightarrow H_1\times H_2$ with $h_1h_2\mapsto(h_1,h_2)$. The map $\varphi$ is well-defined, because for any $h_1,k_1\in H_1$ and $h_2,k_2\in H_2$, $h_1h_2=k_1k_2$ implies $h_1=k_1$ and $h_2=k_2$; this is because $k_1^{-1}h_1=k_2h_2^{-1}\in H_1\cap H_2=\{e\}$. To show that $\varphi$ is a group homomorphism, we need to show that $(h_1h_2)(k_1k_2)=h_1k_1h_2k_2$, which is equivalent to $k_1$ and $h_2$ commuting, that is, $k_1h_2k_1^{-1}h_2^{-1}=e$. To prove this, note that $k_1h_2k_1^{-1}h_2^{-1}=(k_1h_2k_1^{-1})h_2^{-1}\in (k_1H_2k_1^{-1})H_2=H_2$, and similarly $k_1(h_2k_1^{-1}h_2^{-1})\in H_1$, so $k_1h_2k_1^{-1}h_2^{-1}\in H_1\cap H_2=\{e\}$. This proves that $\varphi$ is a group homomorphism. It is clearly a bijection, so it is an isomorphism, that is, $G\cong H_1\times H_2$.

In a direct product, elements of the two factors always commute, because $hg=(e_G,h)(g,e_H)=(g,h)=gh$. So if both factors are Abelian groups, the direct product must also be an Abelian group.

Not every group can be written as the direct product of two nontrivial subgroups.

???+ example "Example: the symmetry group $D_6$ of an equilateral triangle (continued)"
    For example, $D_6=\langle r,s\rangle$ is not isomorphic to $\langle r\rangle\times\langle s\rangle$, because the latter, as a direct product of two cyclic groups, must be Abelian.

The classification theorem below shows that every finitely generated Abelian group can be written as a direct product of finitely many cyclic groups.

### Classification theorem

For finitely generated Abelian groups, there is the following classification theorem. It is called the **fundamental theorem of finitely generated Abelian groups**.

???+ note "Fundamental theorem of finitely generated Abelian groups"
    For a finitely generated Abelian group $G$, there exist an integer $r\ge0$ and integers $n_1,\cdots,n_s\ge 2$ such that

    $$
    G\cong C_\infty^r\times C_{n_1}\times\cdots\times C_{n_s}.
    $$

    In particular, $r$ is uniquely determined and is called the **rank** of $G$. Moreover,

    -   the integers $n_1,\cdots,n_s$ can be chosen to satisfy $n_1\ge2,\ n_1|n_2,\ \cdots,\ n_{s-1}|n_s$. In that case, the integers $n_1,\cdots,n_s$ are uniquely determined, and the factors $C_{n_i}$ are called the **invariant factors** of $G$;
    -   the integers $n_1,\cdots,n_s$ can also be chosen to all be prime powers. In that case, these prime powers are also uniquely determined, and the factors $C_{n_i}$ are called the **elementary divisors** of $G$.

The theorem first asserts that a finitely generated Abelian group must be a direct product of finitely many cyclic groups.

??? note "Proof"
    Here we give a proof that is simple in form[^proof-abelian]; for a deeper proof, see the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain[^module-pid]. In the proof, for ease of writing, we use additive notation instead of the usual multiplicative notation for groups: $0$ denotes the identity, and $mx$ denotes the $m$-th power of $x$.

    Let $k$ be the smallest number of elements that can generate $G$. The proof is by induction on $k$. The case $k=1$ is trivial. When $k>1$, among all generating tuples $(x_1,x_2,\cdots,x_k)$ of $G$, choose one where the order of $x_1$ is smallest. We will show that $G=\langle x_1\rangle\times\langle x_2,\cdots,x_k\rangle$. By the induction hypothesis, the latter already splits into a direct product of $(k-1)$ cyclic groups, which completes the induction step.

    By the characterization of direct products, if the direct product decomposition fails, there must be a relation $m_1x_1+m_2x_2+\cdots+m_kx_k=0$ with $m_1x_1\neq 0$. For a negative coefficient $m_i$, we can replace $x_i$ with its inverse $-x_i$, so all coefficients $m_i$ can be taken to be nonnegative integers. Also, we can then take $0< m_1 <|x_1|$. If we further let $d=\gcd(m_1,m_2,\cdots,m_k)$ and $c_i=m_i/d$, then $y_1=c_1x_1+c_2x_2+\cdots+c_kx_k$ satisfies $dy_1=0$, so $|y_1|\le d\le m_1< |x_1|$; that is, $y_1$ has smaller order than $x_1$.

    Next we prove that $y_1$ can be extended to a generating set of $G$. That is, there exist elements $y_2,\cdots,y_k\in G$ with $G=\langle y_1,y_2,\cdots,y_k\rangle$. The only thing we know here is that $y_1$ itself can be written as $c_1x_1+c_2x_2+\cdots+c_kx_k$, where the coefficients $c_i$ are natural numbers with greatest common divisor one. Without loss of generality, assume the coefficients are in (non-strictly) decreasing order. Then $y_1$ can also be written as $(c_1-c_2)x_1+c_2(x_1+x_2)+\cdots+c_kx_k$. Compared with the earlier conditions, the coefficients are still all natural numbers with greatest common divisor one, and $G=\langle x_1,x_1+x_2,x_3,\cdots,x_k\rangle$, but the sum of all coefficients has decreased by $c_2$. If $c_2=0$, then we must have $c_1=1$, and the result is trivial; otherwise, the sum of the coefficients has strictly decreased. This means that by induction on the sum of the coefficients, we can prove that any $y_1$ satisfying the conditions above can always be extended to a generating set of $G$.

    So we have found a generator $y_1$ with smaller order than $x_1$, which contradicts the choice of $x_1$. Therefore the direct product decomposition must hold, and by the principle of induction, the claim is proved.

Of course, a cyclic group may have infinite or finite order; these give the $C_\infty$ part and the $C_{n_i}$ part of the decomposition above, respectively. Next, the theorem describes the structure of finite cyclic groups $C_n$. The elementary divisor part of the theorem actually relies on the following observation.

???+ note "Lemma"
    If $m$ and $n$ are coprime, then $C_{mn}\cong C_m\times C_n$.

??? note "Proof"
    Let $x$ and $y$ be generators of $C_m$ and $C_n$ respectively. Since $(m,n)=1$, we have $|(x,y)|=mn=|C_m\times C_n|$. So $(x,y)$ is a generator of $C_m\times C_n$, that is, $C_m\times C_n=\langle(x,y)\rangle$. So it is a cyclic group of order $mn$, and must be isomorphic to $C_{mn}$.

Given a cyclic group $C_{n}$, if $n=p_1^{r_1}\cdots p_k^{r_k}$ by the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, then applying the lemma repeatedly shows that $C_n\cong C_{p_1^{r_1}}\times\cdots\times C_{p_k^{r_k}}$. After these two decomposition steps, we have in fact obtained the elementary divisor decomposition in the theorem. Then, using the lemma again to regroup these cyclic groups of prime power order so that their orders meet the requirements, we get the invariant factor decomposition in the theorem. The uniqueness in the theorem can be proved by induction.

??? example "Example: classification of Abelian groups of order 24"
    As an example, the theorem tells us that there are exactly three Abelian groups of order 24, listed below.

    |           Invariant factor decomposition          |                Elementary divisor decomposition               |
    | :-----------------------: | :---------------------------------: |
    |          $C_{24}$         |         $C_{3}\times C_{8}$         |
    |    $C_{2}\times C_{12}$   |     $C_2\times C_{3}\times C_4$     |
    | $C_2\times C_2\times C_6$ | $C_2\times C_2\times C_2\times C_3$ |
