## Introduction

The **15-puzzle** (also called the Gem Puzzle, Boss Puzzle, Game of 15, Mystic Square, N-puzzle, etc.) is a sliding puzzle. The board is $4\times 4$ squares. 15 positions hold numbered tiles in a shuffled order, and the remaining one is empty. A tile in the same row or column as the empty space can be moved by sliding it horizontally or vertically. The goal of the puzzle is to arrange the tiles in numerical order.

The 15-puzzle is often called the **n-puzzle** in general, where the number $n$ is the total number of tiles on the board. Variants of different sizes use similar names; for example, the $8$-puzzle means $8$ tiles on a $3\times3$ board. But the $15$-puzzle can also be called the $16$-puzzle, where 16 refers to the number of cells. Its generalizations sometimes also include $n \times m$ sliding boards.

The 15-puzzle is a classic problem for modeling [heuristic algorithms](../search/heuristic.md). Common heuristics for this problem are the [Manhattan distance](../geometry/distance.md#manhattan-distance) and the number of misplaced tiles. Both are admissible heuristics, meaning they never overestimate the number of remaining moves. This guarantees optimality for certain search algorithms (such as the [A \* algorithm](../search/astar.md)).

???+ note "Notes"
    A **sliding puzzle** is a type of puzzle where you slide pieces on a flat board to reach a certain arrangement. Common sliding puzzles include number puzzles, Klotski (Huarong Dao) and Rush Hour. The 15-puzzle is the oldest sliding puzzle. It was invented by Noyes Chapman and became a craze in the 1880s. Unlike other tour-type puzzles, sliding puzzles never let any piece leave the board, which sets them apart from rearrangement puzzles.

## Definition

Given a $4 \times 4$ board with $15$ tiles in random order, we need to arrange them by number as shown in the figure below. In each move, you may only swap the empty square with one tile next to it. Common questions are: find the minimum number of moves that solves the puzzle, count the number of misplaced tiles, or decide whether the final sorted arrangement can be reached at all.

## Proof of solvability

Johnson & Story (1879) proved that if $m$ and $n$ are both at least $2$, the converse holds for boards of size $m\times n$: by induction on $m$ and $n$ starting from $m=n=2$, every even permutation is solvable. Archer (1999) gave another proof, based on defining equivalence classes via Hamiltonian paths.

## Algorithm

Finding some solution to a sliding number puzzle is relatively easy, but finding an **optimal solution** is **NP-hard**. An optimal solution of the 15-Puzzle needs at most 80 moves, and an optimal solution of the 8-Puzzle needs at most 31 moves.

The N-Puzzle can be solved with common graph search algorithms, such as breadth-first search and depth-first search. We can also use the [A \* search](../search/astar.md) algorithm to find an optimal solution. The heuristic function $h(n)$ can be

-   the number of misplaced tiles;
-   the sum of the Euclidean distances from each misplaced tile to its target position;
-   the sum of the Manhattan distances from each misplaced tile to its target position.

### Group theory

Because the configurations of the 15-tile sliding puzzle can be generated by 3-cycles, one can prove that the 15-tile puzzle can be represented by the alternating group $A_{15}$. In fact, any sliding puzzle using $2\times k-1$ square tiles of equal size can be represented by the alternating group $A_{2k-1}$.
